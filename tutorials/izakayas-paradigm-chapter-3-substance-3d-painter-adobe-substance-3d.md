**Note on format:** unlike the rest of this library, this is an artist-philosophy piece (French-language interview, translated below), not a step-by-step software tutorial. It is Chapter 3 of Adobe's multi-part "Izakaya's Paradigm" project-breakdown series (the other chapters cover Substance 3D Designer/Modeler/Stager and are out of scope for this Painter-only skill). There is almost no literal UI-action transcript to extract — the video is mostly artist-interview B-roll (talking-head shots, hand-painting a physical traditional miniature as an analogy, finished-render cutaways) with one brief real Painter screen-capture shot. Key Steps below are the artist's stated working *principles*, not a literal click-sequence.

### Core Technique
Layer-stacked digital "patina" philosophy: build a technically complex procedural base pass in Painter first, then hand-paint a distinct, deliberately-visible 2D-illustration-style layer on top of it — flat colors, visible brush marks, and small "baked-in" light-like highlights that are part of the texture itself rather than generated by the renderer — so texturing and lighting design happen together, iteratively, right up through final render.

### Summary
The artist (working on the "Izakaya" project, a stylized Japanese-tavern-themed piece) describes Substance 3D Painter as the tool that lets him color previously-sculpted/modeled work through a layer stack conceptually similar to traditional physical patina work — washes, brushing, using recesses and raised details ("creux"/"bosses") to drive where texture, grain, metal, transparency, and emission apply. A key technique named explicitly: sampling/photographing real-world textures (slightly painted wood with visible brush marks, wallpaper, concrete, "anything") and even importing actual Photoshop brushes into Painter to reuse familiar 2D brush libraries in a 3D texturing context. He states there's no hard limit on how many layers can be stacked — the practical constraint is export resolution, which depends entirely on the downstream use case (real-time game engine vs. a static image vs. an animation) and how much render time is acceptable. For this particular project he had no hard technical constraints beyond wanting a visually pleasing 3D render, so heavier textures and longer compute times were an acceptable trade-off. Stylistically, he deliberately avoided full realism, aiming instead for a middle ground between flat 2D illustration and dimensional volume: a procedurally-built, tileable base pass first (leaning on Painter's procedural-texture strength to cover complex areas quickly and consistently), then a hand-painted pass on top — flat color blocks, visible "pencil-mark"-like brush traces, and small texture-baked highlights (not renderer-generated) — to keep a visibly handmade, one-of-a-kind quality. Crucially, he treats texturing and lighting/render-look as co-developed rather than sequential: even after producing what he thought were final renders, he goes back into the texture (a specific detail, a tone that "doesn't work"), updates the file, re-sends/re-renders, and iterates until satisfied — texture and lighting evolve together across the whole project timeline, not in one clean handoff.

### Key Steps (stated working principles, not literal UI clicks)
1. Build color/material work in Painter on top of previously completed sculpting/modeling — Painter is explicitly framed as the "coloring" stage of the pipeline, analogous to traditional patina application.
2. Use recesses and raised surface detail (curvature/height-driven masking, in practice — described here as "creux"/"bosses") to control where texture, grain, metal response, transparency, and emission apply, rather than painting everything uniformly by hand.
3. Sample/derive textures from real-world references (painted wood with visible brushwork, wallpaper, concrete, etc.) as a source of authentic surface detail.
4. Import actual Photoshop brushes into Painter's brush library so familiar 2D brush behavior carries over into 3D texturing work.
5. Don't self-limit layer count during the creative process — treat the layer stack as effectively unbounded and only think about consolidation/resolution at export time.
6. Choose texture-set resolution based on the actual downstream target (game engine real-time budget vs. a one-off static image vs. animation) rather than a fixed default — when there's no hard technical constraint, favor visual quality over compute time.
7. Build a technically complex, tileable **procedural base layer** first — leaning on Painter's procedural-generator strength to cover large/complex areas consistently and quickly.
8. On top of that base, hand-paint a deliberately more 2D/illustrative pass: flat color blocks ("aplats"), visible hand-painted marks to bring out specific details, and small highlight/glow-like details baked directly into the texture (not left to the render engine) to preserve a visibly handmade, unique quality that adds perceived value to the work.
9. Treat texture creation and lighting/render look as a continuous, iterative loop rather than sequential stages — after an apparently "final" render, go back and adjust a texture detail whose tone or read isn't working, re-export, and re-render until satisfied.

### Layers / Tools / Settings
- Layer stack (effectively unbounded layer count during work-in-progress)
- Curvature/height-driven masking (recesses vs. raised detail) to target texture/grain/metal/transparency/emission application
- Real-world texture sampling/photo-reference as a source material
- Imported Photoshop brushes for hand-painting
- Procedural base layer(s) followed by a hand-painted illustrative pass
- Emissive/highlight detail baked into the texture itself (not renderer-driven lighting)
- Export resolution chosen per downstream target (game engine / static image / animation)
